## 1. The short version (the disclaimer)

**clausecraft is a research and portfolio project. It is not legal advice,
and it does not create an attorney–client relationship of any kind.**

Every classification, deviation flag, redline suggestion, summary, or
"verdict" produced by clausecraft is a **draft generated by a language
model, against a public-source reference playbook.** The model can be
wrong. The model can be confidently wrong. The model can miss things that
matter and over-flag things that do not. The playbook is a reference
set, not a legal authority.

**You are the decision-maker.** clausecraft is a tool to speed up the
mechanical parts of contract review — clause extraction, citation
retrieval, formatting of redlines. It is not a substitute for a
qualified attorney, a paralegal supervised by one, or your own judgment
if you are the operator.

If clausecraft cannot find a playbook clause to compare your contract
against, it flags the deviation as **"unverified"** rather than asserting
it. That is the central safety mechanism: we mark what we cannot cite,
and we never pretend unverified is verified.

Use those unverified flags as a prompt to think harder, not as a
conclusion.

## 2. When clausecraft is appropriate

clausecraft is designed for three use cases. These are the ones where the
system can actually help a human reviewer.

1. **Early triage.** A reviewer gets a contract on their desk and wants
   a fast first pass to know where to focus. The deviation table is a
   reading list, not a verdict.
2. **Deviation review against a known baseline.** The reviewer already
   knows what "house style" looks like (NDAs, DPAs, employment contracts
   in the supported types) and wants to see where this contract departs
   from it, with the citation that justifies the flag.
3. **Learning tool.** A junior reviewer, law student, or engineer
   learning contract review reads the deviation table alongside the
   playbook to see *why* a given clause is a deviation — what the
   baseline says, and what the alternative is.

In all three, **a human reviews the output and signs off.** The system
never produces a signable document by itself.

## 3. When clausecraft is **not** appropriate

The system is **not** designed for, and must not be used for:

1. **Live deal-signing.** Do not let a counterparty's contract flow
   through clausecraft and then sign the redlined output without a
   human lawyer reading the redline end-to-end. The redline is a
   draft; signing it as-is means you signed a draft.
2. **Regulated industries without human review.** Insurance, banking,
   healthcare, defense, public sector — anything with statutory review
   requirements — must have a qualified attorney in the loop. The
   "not legal advice" line is not a substitute for the regulatory
   chain of custody.
3. **Jurisdiction-specific legal opinions.** clausecraft knows about
   EU SCCs, IAPP DPA references, BGB § 305 ff. AGB-Kontrolle, DSGVO,
   and similar public sources. It does **not** know the local case law
   of a specific German Landgericht, a US state, a UK court, or an
   arbitration body. A "verdict" on enforceability, validity, or
   governing-law scope is outside what this system can answer.

If the user is unsure whether their case is in scope 2.1–2.3 or
3.1–3.3, the answer is **3.** Treat the contract as out of scope and
hand it to a human reviewer.

## 4. Limitations

The honest version of what the system can and cannot do today.

- **Dev set is small.** 25 contracts × 2 languages (EN, DE) in the
  evaluation set. Numbers below are smoke-run F1 scores on that set,
  not production-quality claims on a general corpus.
- **Classification F1 (smoke runs):** 0.74 EN, 0.67 DE. The system
  misclassifies contract type on roughly a quarter of out-of-set
  contracts. A wrong classification short-circuits the rest of the
  pipeline — the deviation table will be nonsense against the wrong
  playbook. The UI surfaces the detected contract type prominently;
  verify it.
- **Deviation F1 (smoke runs):** 1.0 EN, 1.0 DE on the eval set. The
  "1.0" is on a small, in-set, hand-golden set — it is **not** a
  production-precision claim. Real contracts deviate in ways the
  eval set does not cover. New deviation types are expected; the
  system is allowed to fail on them, and is required to mark them
  unverified.
- **Counterparty matrix is opinion-based.** The "acceptable /
  material / unacceptable" verdicts in Phase 5 reflect a stated
  house position, not a market consensus or a legal finding. Two
  lawyers can disagree; the matrix encodes one of them.
- **Languages: EN and DE only.** Anything else returns a graceful
  error at ingest.
- **No PDF round-trip.** Ingest is `.docx` and `.txt`. PDF is a
  separate pipeline and is not in scope for v1.
- **No multi-tenant auth, no SaaS, no persistent customer contracts.**
  See `docs/09-threat-model.md` § "What we don't handle." An upload
  is one-shot; the audit log is anonymized and is the operator's
  responsibility to export.
- **Self-check loop oscillation.** The redline drafter re-runs the
  deviation spotter on its own output. That loop is capped at one
  retry; if the redline still flags, the system surfaces the
  conflict to the user rather than silently picking a side. See
  `docs/11-phases.md` § "Risks" (line 433).

The system is allowed to fail. The contract is that **failures are
visible, not silent** — every flag carries a citation, every
un-citable claim is marked unverified, every download leaves an
audit-log row.

## 5. License and data handling

- **Code license.** Apache 2.0. See `LICENSE` at the repo root.
- **Playbook license.** Each clause in the playbook is sourced from a
  public reference (EU SCCs, IAPP, ABA, BGB, DSGVO, Tarifvertrag,
  WKO FEEI, etc.) with provenance attached at `playbook/baselines/<type>-<lang>/SOURCES.md`.
  The playbook is a reference set, not a legal authority. The system
  is reproducible against a frozen playbook version.
- **Clause input handling.** Contract text uploaded for review is
  processed locally by the single-host stack. It is **not** stored
  beyond the in-flight review session, and the contract body is
  **not** persisted in the audit log (only the operation metadata:
  contract type, language, deviation count, severity histogram, the
  operator id, and the action taken). Exporting the full audit log
  — including the anonymized metadata — is the operator's
  responsibility, not the system's.
- **Langfuse tracing is opt-in.** If `LANGFUSE_ENABLED=true` in the
  environment, LLM prompts and responses are sent to the self-hosted
  Langfuse instance for observability. The default in
  `docker-compose.yml` is off. If a deployment turns it on, the
  operator is responsible for the data-handling implications of
  that decision — the Langfuse instance is part of the same stack,
  but it does retain the prompts and responses.
- **No third-party LLM data-sharing claim.** The base LLM is reached
  via OpenRouter (configured in `docker-compose.yml`). OpenRouter's
  own data-handling terms apply on top of this disclaimer; the
  operator is responsible for understanding them before turning
  tracing on for contracts that contain sensitive text.
